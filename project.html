Modern Major League Baseball analytics has been revolutionized by Hawk-Eye optical tracking and bat-sensor Statcast data provided through Baseball Savant. While traditional metrics focused solely on pitch velocity and movement, newly available swing-tracking technology captures the 3D trajectory of the bat barrel through the hitting zone. This project is specifically focused on how a hitter’s bat swing path—in particular, their vertical attack angle and swing plane through the zone—shapes contact quality and dictates batted-ball outcomes.

A hitter’s swing path is the fundamental blueprint for ball flight. A swing path that is too steep or too flat frequently leads to rolled-over ground balls or weak pop-ups. Conversely, an optimized bat path that closely mirrors the downward approach angle of incoming pitches creates a wider window of square contact, driving balls into the line-drive and high-value “barrel” zones. In this project, I examine how bat swing path metrics interact with pitch characteristics to produce outs, singles, extra-base hits, and home runs across elite MLB batters.

ImportantCore Research Question

How does a hitter’s bat swing path (specifically their vertical attack angle) interact with pitch type to shape launch angle and exit velocity, and what swing paths consistently maximize extra-base hits and home runs?

The data for this analysis comes from MLB Statcast via Baseball Savant, where each observation represents an individual pitch put into play. Below is a summary of the core variables I use to model and explain swing outcomes:

Why These Variables Matter Together:

  • Bat Path Attack Angle (attack_angle): Dictates how cleanly the barrel plane matches the downward flight of the pitch. An upward path of \(8^\circ\text{--}18^\circ\) maximizes line-drive and extra-base hit launch angles.
  • Exit Velocity (launch_speed) & Launch Angle (launch_angle): Higher exit velocities give fielders less time to react and carry the ball farther, but hard contact hit straight into the ground (\(< 5^\circ\)) often results in an out. Pairing \(98+\text{ mph}\) exit velocity with an optimal launch angle (\(24^\circ\text{--}38^\circ\)) defines the Barrel Zone, where home runs are most frequent.
  • Bat Speed (bat_speed): Introduced in recent Statcast tracking, bat speed separates a batter’s raw swing effort from point-of-contact quality.
  • Pitch Type (pitch_type) & Release Velocity (release_speed): Fastballs provide incoming kinetic energy that can boost exit velocity when squared up, whereas breaking and offspeed pitches disrupt timing and induce weaker launch angles.

Key Insights from the League-Wide Batted-Ball Distribution

  • The Power of the Barrel Zone (\(24^\circ\text{--}38^\circ\), \(98+\text{ mph}\)): Notice the tight cluster of red Home Runs concentrated inside the shaded rectangle. Balls struck with both elite exit velocity and an optimal launch angle produce an expected slugging percentage near \(2.000\), resulting in nearly all home run production in the 2025 dataset.
  • Line Drives Drive Batting Average (\(10^\circ\text{--}25^\circ\)): The teal points (Singles) and orange points (Extra-Base Hits) dominate this corridor. Line drives regularly travel over the heads of the infielders while landing in the gaps ahead of outfielders, maximizing on-base probability.
  • The Ground Ball Trap (\(< 10^\circ\)): Even when a batter makes blisteringly hard contact (\(100+\text{ mph}\)) into the ground, turf friction and infielder positioning limit the outcome to in-play outs or routine singles. Downward contact almost never produces extra bases.
  • Pop-Ups and Ineffective Lift (\(> 50^\circ\)): Regardless of how hard the ball is struck, balls launched at extreme upward angles hang in the air for 5+ seconds, allowing fielders to settle underneath them for routine outs (\(> 98\%\) out rate).
NoteTakeaway for Batter Approach

Maximizing offensive production is not simply a matter of hitting the ball hard—it requires directing kinetic energy into the \(10^\circ\text{ to }35^\circ\) launch window where ball flight physics and field dimensions yield the greatest offensive run expectancy.

When Baseball Savant added Hawk-Eye bat tracking to Statcast, it revealed a swing mechanic that traditional box-score stats could never see: Attack Angle (attack_angle), the vertical direction in degrees that the bat’s sweet spot is traveling at the exact moment it meets the baseball (0° is perfectly level with the ground, positive angles move upward, and negative angles chop downward). Across the 1,405 tracked balls in play in our 2025 Statcast dataset featuring Aaron Judge, Bobby Witt Jr., Kyle Stowers, and Adley Rutschman, plotting every swing’s attack angle against its resulting exit velocity and batted-ball outcome exposes a clear offensive “Goldilocks” zone.

The static chart above shows that swings meeting the ball on a 10° to 20° upward path generate by far the most destructive contact in baseball. Inside the 10°–15° and 15°–20° bands, average exit velocity peaks at 94.5 mph, barrel rates climb to 15.6%–17.7%, extra-base hits jump to 22.2%–24.0%, and hitters post a massive .531 to .575 wOBA on balls in play (11.4%–14.6% home runs). Outside that window, production falls off in two completely different ways: downward chops below 0° beat the ball into the turf (1.9° average launch angle), yielding 32.6% singles but an almost nonexistent 0.9% home run rate, whereas overly steep uppercuts above 20° slice underneath the baseball—causing average exit velocity to plunge nearly 9 mph to 85.5 mph and barrels to collapse from 17.7% down to 5.7%.

The physics diagram above puts this sweet spot into context: because a pitcher releases the baseball from an elevated mound, nearly every fastball and breaking ball crosses home plate moving downward at 6° to 10°. A hitter whose barrel rises at 10° to 20° meets that descending pitch nearly head-on, keeping the sweet spot aligned with the ball’s path across a wider timing window instead of grazing it at a single point. That explains the distinct player archetypes in our dataset: power hitters Aaron Judge (11.0° average attack angle, .583 wOBA) and Kyle Stowers (10.0°, .518 wOBA) live right at the front door of the 10°–20° damage zone, while Bobby Witt Jr. (5.2°, .422 wOBA) intentionally trades loft (4.6% home runs per ball in play) for a flatter line-drive stroke that lets his elite bat speed (74.6 mph) and sprint speed turn hard grounders and low liners into extra bases.
